This topic explains the role of cybersecurity frameworks (CSFs) and regulatory standards in guiding information security protection.

Cybersecurity frameworks
--------------------------------------

Cybersecurity frameworks (CSFs) are capability frameworks used for the advancement of holistic information security protection. They are not a silver bullet for successful cybersecurity programs, but they are extremely helpful in framing your direction and the controls you should put in place. They inform you of the technical and procedural controls that you should put in place to protect your organizations, and in the case of identity security, they typically include the recommended controls surrounding access management, multifactor authentication, privilege controls, auditing, and accountability.

CSFs provide a holistic scope of security coverage, including identity, vulnerability management, patching, and more. They include the classification and categorization of controls, occasionally with recommended order or maturity. Commonly used cybersecurity frameworks include NIST CSF 2.0, CIS CSC V8, CSA CMM and HITRUST, among others.

However, CSFs do have their shortcomings as well. They don’t always tell you where to focus your attention on in terms of their implementation. They don’t account for the variety of different identities or the enterprise resources they access in your organizations. Most don’t explain how to mature your organizational processes in a meaningful or productive way. This is where area-specific frameworks such as the CyberArk Blueprint can help.

Regulatory standards
----------------------------------

Regulatory standards are often misconstrued as cybersecurity frameworks, and while similar, they have some major differences. CSFs are not typically mandatory and consist of recommendations and guidelines that organizations should strive for, which enable benchmarks. Many Security Leaders treat CSFs as a guiding light akin to regulations.

Regulatory standards are often derivatives of CSFs, but it is mandatory to abide by them. Governing bodies, whether governmental or industry-specific, develop strict regulations and it is mandatory to be compliant with them. Organizations are subject to monetary fines or penalties for non-compliance.

Common regulatory standards include NIST 800-171 for CUI, CMMC 2.0, NIST 800-53 for FISMA, PCI DSS, SOX, HIPPA, among others.

Cyber insurance underwriting
------------------------------------------

Cyber insurance underwriting requirements are often treated or viewed as a regulatory standard but are an opt-in choice for organizations seeking cyber insurance. Many of their requirements are derived from trusted cybersecurity frameworks, in addition to individual requirements from various insurance companies, based on their most recent payouts.

Common controls and requirements
----------------------------------------------

As you examine the cybersecurity frameworks, regulatory compliance standards, and cyber-insurance requirements, you'll start to see a common thread. While they all have subtle variations in identity security-related topics, the themes are universal. They all contain requirements for:

*   Access and lifecycle management - Manage access centrally. Ensure that only authorized identities can access systems and data. Establish processes to grant/revoke access at the time of hire or a role change.

*   Multi-factor authentication - Require more than one method of authentication to verify the identity of a user accessing sensitive information or systems. Cover remote and local network and system access.

*   Intelligent privilege controls - Limit user access rights to the minimum necessary. Manage and protect administrative privileges, workstations, and infrastructure. Monitor and control privileged sessions. Enforce separation of duties.

*   Accountability, auditing, and ITDR - Collect and retain audit logs for reporting identity and system activity. Correlate audit review and analysis for reporting purposes. Monitor for anomalous behavior.

Identity security provides security controls and processes for all of these common identity-related requirements in cybersecurity frameworks, regulatory standards, and cyber-insurance. It covers all identities, all resources, the proactive and reactive controls, the lifecycle, and the processes. However, as you’ll see in the next section, there are issues with exclusively relying on these frameworks and standards as your guide to security.
