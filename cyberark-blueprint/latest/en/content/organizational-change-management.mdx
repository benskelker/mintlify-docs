This topic defines organizational change management (OCM), explains its relevance to identity security programs, and outlines key techniques for effectively managing change.

What is organizational change management?
------------------------------------------------------

Organizational change management (OCM) is the process of managing the effects and implications of new business processes, changes in organizational structure, or cultural changes within an enterprise. Essentially, when an organization introduces a new IT solution or any other major change, it's not just the systems and processes that change, but the way people within the organization work. OCM addresses this human aspect of the changes.

Effective OCM helps to smooth the transition from old to new, minimizing disruptions and resistance from employees, streamlining the rollout and consumption of the new IT service, and ensuring that the change achieves its intended outcomes.

Why OCM is relevant to identity security-related programs
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Identity security is all about applying the right level of intelligent privilege control to the right identity (user) in the right situation (privilege and enterprise resource), which means that we’re changing the way users are interacting with their resources. We’re introducing new methods of access, new systems, and new processes, and all of this can impact the user experience from their current baseline.

Techniques you should consider implementing as part of your OCM initiative
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

*   Communication strategy and stakeholder alignment: This involves communicating the change to all stakeholders in a clear and timely manner to ensure that everyone understands why the change is happening, what the change will involve, and how it will benefit the organization. It includes identify all stakeholders affected by the change and actively involve them in the change process, from planning through to implementation, to ensure that their needs are met and their feedback is considered.

*   Top-down consistent messaging: This sets the tone for the entire initiative from an end-user perspective, and it starts with having active and visible leadership across IT and Security. This helps foster partnership among other Line of Business senior leaders and drives user buy-in. Users are more likely to buy into the changes when their own leaders are out in front with the change. When all your leaders are speaking the same language and positioning the same message, whether your end-user hears the CISO or the VP of their department speak, they should be hearing the same thing.

*   Change leadership: Having a strong leadership team to drive the change and act as champions of the new approach. Leaders play a critical role in motivating staff and modeling behaviors expected in the new environment. From there, you can begin to develop internal champions to test use cases, provide input, and help support users post go-live (be your early adopters). This will help drive consistent messaging at a peer-level. To drive all that messaging, we recommend having proactive email communication to keep users informed and setting up a mailbox available for questions. Those emails can be delivered in the form of monthly newsletters to raise awareness across the organization, and perhaps you can find a sample newsletter that you can leverage for content or structure.

*   User enablement and training: Whenever asking people to do something new or different, make sure you’re giving them the proper training to do so. It’s highly recommended to provide your end-users mandatory training for whatever identity security initiatives you’re building so that they at least become familiar with the processes. It’s important to put yourself in that user persona’s shoes and to develop the training from that persona’s perspective. There are many great ways to develop training, including publishing short video clips with quiz questions in your internal learning management system, defining clear internal escalation and support processes with an end user guide, and creating a self-serve resource portal to provide users quick access to resources and materials. In addition to the “how to” guidance, it’s also important to publish your security control framework to make it easy and clear for end-users to know why these security controls apply to them.

*   Gamification: When appropriate, consider turning usage of new cybersecurity controls into a game or competition. Running contests to promote adoption and engagement of new controls is an engaging way of getting users interested in using these new controls. Think about what “carrots” have been used successfully within your organization in the past, like branded company swag or training vouchers. Equally important is monitoring usage and following up on it to increase compliance.

*   Empower operations: It’s important to make the end-users feel more empowered in how the service is run. When possible, to leverage a single request system for all identity security and access-related requests, avoid using multiple request systems and making the process complicated. Your overall service delivery becomes more cohesive, streamlined, and easier to use. Automate the delivery of access and onboarding requests, whether access to a corporate web app, the onboarding of a privileged account, or a developer requesting the provisioning of machine identity. Make it easier, faster, and more accurate for the end-users. Consider publishing a service status page where users can check status, uptime, and maintenance information. End-users always want to blame a new solution when it has changed the way they work and having a service status page offers transparency, showing that services are operating as expected.

*   Feedback mechanisms: Implement ways to gather feedback from employees on the change process and use this information to adjust and improve the change strategies. This enables you to put your finger on the “pulse of the people” as it were. Depending on the size and scale of the initiative, consider facilitating an Executive All-Hands meeting include leadership messaging, live demos, and opportunities for the user communities to ask questions. This type of activity is best suited prior to going live with your new security controls. On the post-go-live side, you can host one time or recurring office hours sessions to provide a live forum for questions post-implementation.
