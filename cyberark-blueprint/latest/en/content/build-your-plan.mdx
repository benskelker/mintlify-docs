This topic outlines the steps for building a custom identity security roadmap using the CyberArk Blueprint.

Now it's time to build your roadmap. The CyberArk Blueprint uses five key factors to start the development of a custom, tailor made, identity security roadmap for each organization: an assessment of your current state, CyberArk Blueprint’s risk-based recommendations, internal priorities and drivers, your bandwidth and capacity, and business outcomes and success metrics.

Being able to navigate these five factors will make your roadmap building exercise significantly easier, so do this homework first. Working to overcome those challenges (if applicable) will increase your odds of success, too. When you put this all together, you can create an identity security roadmap that’s specific to your organization but is still reflective of industry and security best practice and has the best odds for success.

We encourage you to check out the [Organizational change management](/cyberark-blueprint/latest/en/content/organizational-change-management) section as you’re building your plan.

Assess your current state
---------------------------------------

You can’t get to your destination without understanding where you are today. A key part of our approach to roadmap development is to take a snapshot of where you are today, capturing your existing security controls, the technologies they are applied to, and the identities they protect. This enables you to make more informed decisions and figure out where you can focus your identity security efforts moving forward.

Begin by reviewing your existing IAM, PAM, and DevOps standards and security policies. Combine this with your inventory reports to verify which identities, systems, accounts, and resources are under specific controls. This will help you gain an accurate understanding of your landscape, as opposed to making assumptions.

Furthermore, organizations can leverage the CyberArk Blueprint’s use case best practice recommendations as a reference for assessing their current state. Review the [self-assessment](/cyberark-blueprint/latest/en/content/assess-security-posture) section to learn more about accelerating the assessment of your identity security state.

Review the CyberArk Blueprint recommendations
-----------------------------------------------------------

The CyberArk Blueprint is only one part of the process to create an identity security roadmap. It’s there to serve as a guide and tool to help you along the way so you can make the most informed decisions about the technologies or use cases you want to secure. Its prescriptive nature helps you identify which use cases provide the most risk reduction relative to the effort required to implement them. This is most relevant when building your medium and long-term roadmaps, which generally span many technologies.

So, when you read CyberArk’s recommendation that “XYZ use case” is a critical risk and “ABC use case” is less critical, know that it’s rooted in our experience and lessons learned that “XYZ” is more impactful from a security and risk mitigation perspective for the level of effort that’s required to accomplish it. That doesn’t mean you still shouldn’t do “ABC use case” first – it means that you’ve now made an informed decision knowing the security implications in that trade off.

Our [Use case best practices](/cyberark-blueprint/latest/en/content/use-case-best-practices) section is a great way to review CyberArk’s use case specific recommendations. These sections provide detailed explanations of which identity security controls should be applied and how to apply them. They also include justifications for why these controls are important. This is useful information for assessing controls and use cases for your roadmap.

Identify your internal priorities and drivers
-----------------------------------------------------------

While the CyberArk Blueprint leverages a risk-reduction first strategy, that’s not always the case for every customer. Everyone enters identity security for a different reason, and it’s completely fine for another factor to drive the order of a roadmap. If an organization has other reasons for prioritization, that’s completely okay, but as good security practitioners and trusted advisors we think it’s important to advise security leaders on why the Blueprint is prescriptive in its manner, help them make intelligent decisions and ultimately make the most informed decisions.

In order to properly assess an order of operations for execution in a roadmap, you need to understand what motivates your organization. Are there audit, compliance, or regulatory requirements? Are you looking to improve operational efficiencies? Are you trying to enable your digital business by providing secure services? Are you trying to simply defend your organization from attacks? Was there an internal security incident or breach? Are there any pet projects by executives?

All of these are all valid reasons for prioritizing use cases over one another but remember, they should all still be weighed against their associated risk, the impact they make, and their level of effort.

Assess your bandwidth and capacity
------------------------------------------------

To determine how much you’re able to do at one time, you’ll need to understand what your organization’s bandwidth and capacity looks like. Is a centralized team responsible for executing everything? Are there multiple teams that can share the responsibility across different security controls or workstreams? Can any of this be delegated to different business units?

Understanding your bandwidth and capacity will determine how much you can do at any given time, and this impacts the total duration of the scope of your roadmap.

Define business outcomes and success metrics
----------------------------------------------------------

You also need to identify your positive business outcomes and goals, and the business end state you want to reach as a result of your initiative. Is it to secure your organization’s cloud workloads? To provide access for your remote workforce to your organization’s resources? Whatever that goal is will also serve as a guiding light for the roadmap design. These business outcomes will often drive the definitions of your success metrics.

As a part of your business outcome planning, it is important to understand the scope of your initiative. Are you building a small, highly targeted roadmap for short term remediation over a few months? Are you targeting a medium-term plan, such as a year-long multi-technology initiative? Or are you planning a long-term, enterprise-wide, large scope roadmap? The larger the scope, the larger the length of time, and the more relevant it becomes to prioritize based on ROI.
